 VHS Camera
 VHS Camera is a free VHS filter for your camera: shoot video and photos that look as if they came off an old camcorder tape, with colour bleed, tape noise and a date stamp. It runs in the browser, so there is no app to install.
 How to film with the VHS filter
 Tap INSERT TAPE and allow the camera, or tap UPLOAD A VIDEO to use a video you already have.
 Choose how worn the tape looks (Clean, Dirty or Worn) and set the date stamp and sound.
 Switch between LIVE and PHOTO, then press the record button.
 Save the clip or photo, or share it straight from the preview.
 VHS filter for your videos
 Already have a clip? Tap UPLOAD A VIDEO on the start screen, or UPLOAD VIDEO in the camera, and pick a video from your phone or computer. It plays with the VHS effect right away, so you can try the tapes, the quality, the date stamp and the sound while you watch. Then save the whole video as an MP4 with its original sound. You don’t need a camera for this, and the video is processed on your device, never uploaded to a server. The sound gets the VHS treatment too: Tape sound, under the Sound button, adds a soft tape hiss, a slight wobble and muffled highs to uploaded videos and LIVE recordings, and one tap turns it off.
 Private: nothing is uploaded
 The VHS effect is calculated on your phone or computer, frame by frame. Your video and photos are never uploaded to a server.
 Frequently asked questions
 Is the VHS filter free?
 Yes. There is no sign-up and nothing to install; it works in the browser.
 Does it work on iPhone and Android?
 Yes, in a current mobile browser such as Safari or Chrome with camera access. On slower phones, the settings let you trade some detail for a smoother frame rate.
 Can I add the VHS effect to a video I already have?
 Yes. Tap UPLOAD A VIDEO, pick an MP4, MOV or WebM file and play it with the effect. Save video then turns the whole clip into an MP4 with the VHS look and the original sound, right in your browser.
 What file do I get?
 Videos are saved as MP4 where the browser supports it (otherwise WebM), photos as JPG.
 Why does it look like a real tape?
 The effect is based on ntsc-rs, which simulates how analogue video is encoded and recorded: colour bleed, tracking noise and soft detail, not a picture laid on top.

> **mrandish** · 2026-11-02T18:35:01.000Z　
> > It doesn’t look like how I remember VHS but I think that ... you probably won’t get useful feedback from people who were around at the time.I agree it's quite tricky to assess and provide accurate feedback but for very different reasons, which I cover below. I disagree about "people who were around at the time" for the simple reason I worked in both video production and creating production software/hardware tools from the mid-80s to the late 2000s and still have VHS, S-VHS, 8mm/Hi8, Laserdisc and 3/4" VTR players hooked up in racks here at home and a half a dozen low-hours CRTs ranging from Sony consumer to BVM and industrial analog RGB arcade monitors (yeah, I'm kind of a retro computer, video and gaming nerd). More importantly I still have a good analog test signal generator, waveform monitor and vectorscope.Why it's hard: It's easy to run synthetic test signals like a zone plate and 8 MHz sweep through a video system and see what's happening quantitatively. The challenge is the target isn't well defined. People's qualitative expectations for "looks like VHS" vary widely, not because of fading memory but because they're using "VHS" to mean more than just the half-inch magnetic tape recording format. Engineering-wise the VHS tape format is largely defined by a fixed bandwidth in megahertz and lines of vertical resolution. But those are theoretical maximums virtually no consumer gear ever achieved. The same tape could look quite a bit different on a really good VHS VCR than the typical Goldstar VCR from Sears. And the tape quality mattered as well as if it was professionally duped or home recorded on a Goldstar and which VHS recording mode was used. It wasn't uncommon that my home recorded VHS tape wouldn't play on my friend's VCR. Tracking was impacted by everything from component quality, calibration, belt wear and temperature. The same professionally mastered VHS tape on my 8-head professional S-VHS deck viewed on a Sony BVM broadcast monitor will look a lot different than it did on your parent's Magnavox VCR and Sylvania TV.When they say "looks like VHS", most people are including composite video encoding and analog cable cross-talk and how their CRT decoded the signal. And in the analog era, all those things could matter a lot in terms of what you saw on screen. I happen to have a box of individually sealed, unopened VHS tapes here that were duped on a professional duplicator directly from my D2 sub-master in 1991 - and I happened to dump some test signals at the end of the master after the client's program. D2 is a digital composite video format sampled at 14.32 Mhz. My pro-VHS VTR can pull a hair over 5 Mhz from one of those tapes. Your Mom's Goldstar VCR probably never pulled more than around 3 Mhz from any tape it ever played and even from one of my tapes, it wouldn't touch 4 Mhz. So, are we targeting what was theoretically possible in the tape standard, what my tape and VCR actually output right now, or what someone remembers from whatever VCR, cables, tape and CRT combo they had in a given year?

> **mrandish** · 2026-11-02T20:54:35.000Z　
> Oh, wow... PAL-N. Been a while since I've heard that term.The resolution of PAL but with the color sub-carrier of NTSC. Your experience was probably quite a bit different than most. On one hand, it might have looked better in some ways but it's likely the range variance of quality across consumer gear was far wider because the market was smaller, so there were fewer standard reference designs and components made to support it. Fortunately, we didn't have to worry about PAL-N on the production equipment side because South American broadcasters produced their content in PAL format and converted to PAL-N at the transmitter. Transcoding PAL to NTSC was hard to do well and even though PAL-N is sort-of half the problem, IIRC, it still wasn't a transparent conversion. Since PAL-N was effectively an over-the-air standard viewable only on TVs made for those regions, I have no idea what it even looked like to home viewers.If there was ever a technology which somehow, against all odds, came into existence decades ahead of its time, it would be analog television. Once you understand how the system actually works... the fact it worked at all in the late 1930s, much less as well as it did seems impossible. It required brilliantly innovative engineering as well as some incredibly elegant hacks and more than a few ugly compromises (the ghosts of which still haunt today's HDTV standards).Then politicians got involved and made this stunning yet impossibly complex, barely working new technology - far worse by splitting it into three different incompatible standards (NTSC, PAL, SECAM). None of them worked with the others and they each spawned their own bizarre variants (NTSC-J, PAL 60, PAL-N, and PALs B, D, G, H, I and K). Broadcast television was both a glorious testament to mankind's ingenuity and our ability to fuck up a good thing for dumb reasons. A tradition that lives on in today's digital television with its truly breathtaking resolution, color depth and fidelity yet ugly artifacts from over-compression, broken DRM and incompatible proprietary tone-mapping standards. :-)
