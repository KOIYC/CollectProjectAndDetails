## Web Development

a trade · 1991 – 2026

W eb Development — trade, craft, and for about fifteen years the most democratic thing going — died at home on a Tuesday, in a browser tab that nobody had open. It was thirty-five.

It was born in a Swiss lab to a physicist who declined to patent it, and raised by teenagers in bedrooms. Its first words were blue and underlined.

It had no credentials and never asked for yours. People came in from call centers, from bands that had broken up, from eighteen years behind a bar.

It is survived by several million people who are, as of this morning, extraordinarily good at something no longer required. They are fine. They keep saying they are fine.

Last words: “Okay — try it now.”

## The Good Times

T here was a committee that existed only to decide where everyone was going on Friday night, and it was the best attended meeting we had. Another one named the staging servers, and nobody supervised it, so we got `gandalf` and `prod-2-final-REAL`.

None of it was ever in the job description.

There was a dog who was not an employee and had a company badge anyway. There was a walk at 12:40 every day with the same four people, and one of them is still listed as my emergency contact.

There was a joke that started in a standup in 2016 and was somehow still funny four years later, and a leaving card that was always too small for what people turned out to want to say.

## The jQuery Years

DO NOT ERASE

```
$(document).ready(function () {
  $('.thing').fadeIn('slow');
});
```

O ne person in the office sat down and learned jQuery properly, over about two weeks, and for the next six years there was a line at his desk every afternoon. He never once seemed to mind it. He is the reason four of us have the careers we have.

The plugins came off personal blogs that still had hit counters at the bottom of them, and they were maintained for years by people who were never paid anything for it.

Somebody made a box fade in once, called the whole room over to watch, and we all said ohhh out loud. We were adults.

## A Day in the Life

Y ou got in at 10:12 am because nobody had ever actually said what time you were supposed to. Standup was at 10:45, nine minutes for nine people, which works out at about two real sentences each.

Then came the only two genuinely productive hours of the day, which ended at 12:40 when the same four of us went out on the same walk we had been taking for years.

The afternoon was a meeting that should have been an email. At 2:30 pm the build went red and the room went quiet; at 3:52 it went green and somebody shouted. At 4:06 everyone left together, and nobody had to suggest it.

## The Office

T he desks were pushed together into one long row, and Dave had the one next to the trash can, which he always claimed he preferred. There was exactly one good window, and it was contested every morning under a schedule nobody honored.

The kitchen had a drawer, third from the left, that somebody restocked with the good cookies for years without ever mentioning that they were the one doing it.

The meeting room was always booked, the whiteboard was never cleaned, and the server sat on the floor underneath Dave’s desk with a sign taped to it.

Do not unplug this is the server

 Every word of this book — the obituary, the jokes, the three portraits, the handwriting on the autographs page — was generated by a language model from a one-line request.
