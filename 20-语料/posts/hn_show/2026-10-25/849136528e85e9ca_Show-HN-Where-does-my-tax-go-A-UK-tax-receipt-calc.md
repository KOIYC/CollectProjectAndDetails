How the split is worked out
Start from the UK totals for each area of spending in 2025-26 (£1227.9bn across ten areas of public services). For the six areas where HM Treasury can attribute spending to a nation (2024-25), each total is scaled by how much more or less is spent per resident in England than the UK average. The results are put back to 100%, and your income tax plus National Insurance is shared out in those proportions. Debt interest is shown separately from the rest of general public services.

What this does and does not show
Your tax is not tied to any one service. Everything is paid into one pot, so a proportional split is an illustration of where the pot goes.
Income tax and employee National Insurance are only part of what the government collects. VAT, corporation tax, council tax, employer National Insurance and other taxes also pay for these services, and the government borrows to cover the gap between spending and receipts.
Total managed expenditure was £1360.1bn in 2025-26. About £132bn of that is accounting adjustments that are not spending on any service, so the shares use the £1227.9bn spent on the ten areas.
Spending by nation: HM Treasury only attributes some spending to a nation (2024-25). Defence, debt interest, other public services, environment and culture are shared across the UK and are not adjusted. Per-resident figures from 2024-25 are applied to earlier and later years too, so treat the nation differences as a guide. Your tax is not ring-fenced to your own nation.
Wales and Northern Ireland use the same income tax bands as England. Only Scotland sets its own bands for earnings. Welsh income tax rates are the UK rates, with the UK government rate reduced and the Welsh Government adding the same amount back.
Student loan repayments come off your take-home pay but are not tax. They go to the Student Loans Company to pay off your loan, so they are left out of the spending split. Payroll works them out each pay period and rounds down, so your payslips may differ by a few pounds.
The calculator ignores pension contributions, benefits in kind, savings and dividends. Use HMRC's own tools for your exact bill. This is an estimate, not tax or financial advice.
Frequently asked questions
 Where does my income tax go?
 Income tax and National Insurance go into one pot that pays for public services. The largest areas are social protection (benefits and pensions, about a third), health (about a fifth), debt interest, education and economic affairs such as transport. Enter your salary above to see your own split in pounds.
 What is the UK Personal Allowance in 2026/27?
 The standard Personal Allowance is £12,570, the amount you can earn before paying income tax. It reduces by £1 for every £2 of income above £100,000 and is zero at £125,140. It is the same for 2024/25 and 2025/26.
 How much National Insurance do employees pay?
 For 2024/25 to 2026/27 the Class 1 employee rate is 8% on earnings between £12,570 and £50,270 a year, and 2% above £50,270.
 Is income tax different in Scotland?
 Yes. Scotland sets its own income tax bands on earnings, with six rates from 19% to 48%. Wales and Northern Ireland use the same bands as England. Select where you live above to see the difference, or see tax by salary in Scotland .
 How much student loan will I repay?
 You repay 9% of your earnings above your plan's threshold, or 6% for a Postgraduate loan. For 2026/27 the yearly thresholds are £26,900 for Plan 1, £29,385 for Plan 2, £33,795 for Plan 4, £25,000 for Plan 5 and £21,000 for Postgraduate loans. Plan 5 repayments start in April 2026. If you have a Postgraduate loan as well as another plan, you repay both. Pick your plan above to see the amount and your take-home pay.
 Which tax year does this tool use?
 You can choose 2026/27, 2025/26 or 2024/25. Spending figures are from HM Treasury outturn data for 2025-26 and 2024-25, and every source is linked at the bottom of the page.
