## Moments

Key events from the mission, each with sources. Choosing one takes you to that moment, and to the observation if there is one.

Early days, 2004–2005

20 November 2004 · 17:16 UTC

#### Launch

Swift launched on a Boeing Delta II from Cape Canaveral at 12:16 p.m. EST. It separated from the rocket about 80 minutes later.

11 December 2004 · 00:45 UTC Observation

#### First X-ray image

The X-ray Telescope's first target was Cassiopeia A, the remains of a supernova.

17 December 2004 · 07:28 UTC Observation

#### First gamma-ray burst

GRB 041217 was the first burst Swift located on board. It could not yet turn to bursts automatically.

27 December 2004

#### Magnetar flare

A month after launch Swift recorded a giant flare from the magnetar SGR 1806-20, about 50,000 light-years away. NASA said it released more energy than the Sun gives out in 150,000 years.

5 April 2005

#### Science begins

After four and a half months of checks, normal science operations started.

9 May 2005 · 04:00 UTC Observation

#### A short burst pinned down

GRB 050509B lasted a fraction of a second. It was the first short burst located quickly and accurately, near an elliptical galaxy at a probable redshift of 0.225. This pointed to two merging compact stars.

4 July 2005 · 07:02 UTC Observation

#### Deep Impact

Swift observed comet 9P/Tempel 1 on the day NASA's Deep Impact probe hit it.

4 September 2005 · 01:51 UTC Observation

#### Distance record

GRB 050904, at redshift 6.29, beat the previous gamma-ray burst record of 4.5.

18 February 2006 · 03:34 UTC Observation

#### A star exploding

GRB 060218 lasted about 2,100 seconds. Its X-rays were read as the shock wave breaking out of a Wolf-Rayet star, which then became the supernova SN 2006aj.

9 January 2008 · 13:32 UTC Observation

#### Supernova caught as it began

While observing another supernova in NGC 2770, 90 million light-years away, the X-ray Telescope recorded a five-minute X-ray flash. It was SN 2008D, seen at the moment it exploded.

19 March 2008 · 06:12 UTC Observation

#### Visible to the naked eye

The optical flash from GRB 080319B reached magnitude 5.3, bright enough to see without a telescope, from 7.5 billion light-years away.

23 April 2009 · 07:55 UTC Observation

#### Most distant explosion

GRB 090423 had a redshift of about 8.2. Its light left when the universe was about 630 million years old.

13 April 2010

#### 500th burst

GRB 100413B was Swift's 500th gamma-ray burst.

28 March 2011 · 12:57 UTC Observation

#### Star torn apart

Swift J1644+57 was a Sun-like star pulled apart by a black hole 3.9 billion light-years away. A jet from the event pointed straight at Earth.

27 April 2013 · 07:47 UTC Observation

#### Record-bright burst

GRB 130427A arrived while Swift was turning between targets. The BAT picked it up less than a minute after Fermi. Its gamma-ray fluence was the highest recorded up to then.

27 October 2015 Observation

#### 1,000th burst

GRB 151027B was Swift's 1,000th gamma-ray burst.

17 August 2017 · 12:41 UTC Observation

#### Neutron star merger

About 15 hours after gravitational waves from merging neutron stars reached Earth, Swift found a fading ultraviolet source in NGC 4993. It was the first ultraviolet light seen from a kilonova.

9 October 2022 · 13:16 UTC Observation

#### Brightest burst on record

Fermi saw GRB 221009A first. Swift's BAT triggered at 14:10 UTC and at first logged it as a new source in our galaxy. It turned out to be the brightest gamma-ray burst ever recorded.

11 August 2007

#### Gyroscope failure

A gyroscope failed on 11 August 2007. Operations were back to normal on 19 October.

6 February 2017

#### Neil Gehrels

Neil Gehrels, Swift's principal investigator since the start, died on 6 February 2017.

10 January 2018

#### Renamed

The Swift Gamma-Ray Burst Explorer was renamed the Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory.

18 January 2022

#### Reaction wheel problem

A reaction wheel fault put Swift in safe mode from 18 January to 17 February 2022.

15 March 2024

#### Two-gyro mode

A noisy gyroscope sent Swift into safe mode on 15 March 2024. A software update let it point using two gyros, and science resumed on 3 April with better pointing than before.

24 September 2025

#### Plan to raise the orbit

Swift's orbit was dropping faster because of high solar activity. NASA gave Katalyst Space Technologies a contract to try to boost it.

17 December 2025

#### Pointing to reduce drag

From December 2025 Swift replaced many targets with pointings that reduced drag. The catalogue lists 57,769 of these 'DragPoint' observations between 17 December 2025 and 26 August 2026.

11 February 2026

#### Science paused

Swift paused most science operations to get ready for the boost.

3 July 2026 · 08:36 UTC

#### LINK launches

Katalyst's LINK spacecraft launched on a Pegasus XL rocket from Kwajalein to meet Swift.

19 August 2026

#### Boost called off

LINK had problems with its reaction wheels and thrusters. NASA announced it would not capture or boost Swift. LINK had come within 12 to 15 km.

26 August 2026

#### Science restarts

Swift restarted observations with its X-ray and UV/optical telescopes. Automatic turning to new bursts came back on 21 September.

20 October 2026 · 18:49 UTC Observation

#### Final observation

On 20 October, at about 275 km, Swift could no longer keep up with the drag. Its last catalogued observation started at 18:49 UTC.

4 November 2026 · 12:39 UTC

#### Re-entry

The U.S. Space Force confirmed that Swift re-entered the atmosphere at 7:39 a.m. EST over North Sumatra, Indonesia, at about 3.4° N, 98.6° E. Most of it was expected to burn up.

## The archive

458,237 observations of 126,070 targets: the full Swift Master Catalog, downloaded 2026-11-06. 0 have images processed for this site, and the rest show the archive's own quick-look images.

## About the data

Everything here comes from public records. This page explains where, how it was worked out, and how accurate it is.

### What this is

NASA's Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory (NORAD 28485, COSPAR 2004-047A) launched on 20 November 2004 and re-entered the atmosphere over North Sumatra on 4 November 2026. This site works out where it was at any moment, what it was pointed at, and for some observations shows the images and measurements it recorded.

### How reliable is the position?

Reconstructed. Calculated from the mission's own orbital elements, using the set closest in time (within 3 days). Older data. The closest set is 3 to 10 days away. This is rare; the longest gap is 20.7 days, in June 2007. Extrapolated. After the last set of elements (20 October 2026). Shown for 36 hours at most. Unavailable. No position is shown. The mission archive has no elements from 21 October until re-entry.

For processed observations, positions come from that observation's own orbit file and pointing from its attitude file. Otherwise the pointing is the catalogue's pointing during a logged exposure. While Swift was turning, the direction is estimated between targets and drawn fainter. The clouds are a NASA composite, not the weather on the day shown.

### Checks

Things we found along the way. The catalogue's start and stop times leave out the spacecraft clock drift. By 2026 they are about 34 seconds late, which would put Swift about 260 km out of place, so every time here is corrected using the mission's clock calibration file. The mission's orbit files also skip precession when they give longitude (about 0.36° off by 2026), so this site works longitude out separately. Older orbit files were timed with the uncorrected clock, and are adjusted for that.
