Missile alert
The source announces a threat from cruise or ballistic missiles. This is the fastest category it announces: a distance a drone covers in an hour, a cruise missile covers in a quarter of one.
What this means for a reader of this page. A missile alert in a border oblast means the window between the announcement and any consequence is short. This page tracks no object and does not know where anything is going; it knows only that this category was announced for that district.
Drone alert
The most frequent category in the records this project keeps. Shahed/Geran-type strike drones, flying slow and low, often in groups and often for many hours of one night.
Why the weather is shown here. Such a drone's track is sensitive to wind, and how much can be seen from the ground depends on precipitation. That is why the weather panel below reports wind on the Polish side.
Glide bombs
Aircraft bombs with a glide kit, released from planes. Their range is short compared with missiles, so alerts in this category occur mainly close to the front line.
What follows from it. A glide bomb announced in Donetsk oblast is a statement about the east, not about danger near the Polish border. It is one of the categories that most mislead a reader who looks only at the number of alerts.
Artillery fire
A front-line category, with a range measured in kilometres or tens of kilometres. In the project's records it is almost entirely the east of the country.
Why it is on this page. Because removing it would change the picture of intensity: a night when the front burns and the west is silent is a different fact from a quiet night, and a reader near the border is entitled to see both.
Alert, type not stated
The source announced an alert but did not name the weapon. This is not a fault of this page and it is not a “lighter” category than the others.
The current source does not name the weapon for airborne alerts: it has one category for everything that flies. Guessing the type from context would be adding information the source did not give, so it stays “unknown”. The source does declare a level, red or yellow. The map fills the area with its colour, but does not infer the weapon from it.
The three silhouettes in this symbol are a key to what the word “unknown” covers: a drone, a glide bomb, a missile. The set is not closed: an alert with no declared kind may equally be a MiG-31K takeoff, a threat from the sea, or something this source never names at all. That is why the bracket under the silhouettes is open, and why three separate icons are not drawn side by side: that would assert “one of these three”, which the source did not say.
A zone activated in PANSA's plan
A piece of sky reserved for a stated time for somebody's planned activity. Ordinary air traffic is kept out or restricted there while it lasts. Sometimes that is training, an exercise or a firing range; sometimes a restriction the state imposes, and on the map they look the same.
A plan, not an alert. A zone does not mean anything is flying over it and does not say what happens in it. Tap an outline to see until when and at what heights it applies.
Archive of falls outside Ukraine
Each mark is one record: something came down outside Ukraine and that country's authorities confirmed it. A burst is a fall with an explosion, a fire or damage. Three shards are fragments or wreckage found, with no explosion. A struck cross is an object shot down over that country's territory. The mark does not say what the object was: the record does not carry it.
The circle is positional uncertainty, not the reach of the event. The mark stands at the place the communique named, and the event was somewhere inside the circle. Nothing here is happening now, and no mark does not mean nothing fell: this is not a complete register.
The icon sits in the middle of the area that announced an alert of that type. It is not the position of an object. The source sees no objects: it sees alerts announced for administrative units. The ring around an icon shows how large that area is, so a district gets a small one and a whole oblast a large one. Nothing here moves, because the source knows no weapon's track.
The five alert types are the ones the source names. Two of them, glide bombs and artillery , are front-line phenomena whose range does not reach the Polish border; they are here because they say which oblast is under fire right now. The distance in the list above is the only place where this page says anything about range.
