 How UK SMEs Can Find New Growth Opportunities Through Public Sector Contracts
For many small and medium-sized businesses, growth usually means finding more private-sector clients, expanding into new markets or increasing sales to existing customers. Yet another sizeable market is often overlooked: supplying goods and services to public-sector organisations.
 SMEs account for 99.9% of the UK business population, according to UK government figures. At the same time, public organisations purchase an enormous range of goods and services every year, creating opportunities for businesses far beyond the large contractors traditionally associated with government work.
 From technology and professional services to construction, training, maintenance, logistics and specialist consultancy, smaller companies may be better positioned to compete for public contracts than many business owners realise.
 Public procurement is becoming more accessible
 For suppliers bidding for opportunities in England, Wales and Northern Ireland, the introduction of the Procurement Act 2023 supplier guidance brought significant changes from 24 February 2025.
 Among the stated aims of the reforms are simplifying procurement processes and reducing unnecessary barriers for SMEs, start-ups and social enterprises. Contracting authorities are also required to consider barriers that may prevent smaller suppliers from participating in relevant procurement exercises.
 For a smaller business, that matters. Public contracts should not automatically be dismissed on the assumption that only companies with large teams, extensive financial resources or years of government contracting experience can participate.
 The opportunity is increasingly about identifying contracts that genuinely match a company's capabilities.
 Start by finding the right opportunities
 One of the biggest mistakes an SME can make is attempting to bid for every vaguely relevant contract.
 Tendering requires time. Preparing documentation, understanding requirements, calculating costs and writing a strong response can consume considerable resources. Businesses therefore need a filtering process before committing to a bid.
 Owners should consider factors such as:
 Whether the contract closely matches their existing products or services
Whether they can realistically meet the required timescales
The geographic area covered by the contract
Required certifications, insurance or professional qualifications
Contract value and available operational capacity
Previous experience that can demonstrate similar results
Whether subcontracting or partnership opportunities are available
Businesses looking for ways to identify and monitor opportunities can also use specialist resources such as Public Sector Tenders to help narrow their search to contracts relevant to their sector and capabilities.
 The objective should not be to find the largest number of tenders. It should be to create a pipeline of opportunities the business has a realistic reason to pursue.
 Prepare before the tender appears
 Successful tendering often begins long before a deadline is announced.
 SMEs can prepare standard company information, case studies, insurance documents, policies and evidence of previous work in advance. This reduces the amount of administrative work required every time a suitable opportunity appears.
 Businesses should also be ready to clearly explain their strengths.
 A smaller supplier may not have the scale of a national corporation, but it may offer specialist expertise, faster communication, greater flexibility or detailed knowledge of a particular market.
 The government's guidance also states that suppliers can store core organisational information through the central digital platform and reuse it when participating in covered procurements, reducing some of the repetitive administrative burden.
 Compete on value, not simply price
 A public-sector tender should not automatically become a race to submit the cheapest possible offer.
 Award criteria can examine a combination of price, quality, experience and other requirements specified by the buyer. Businesses therefore need to read each tender carefully and respond directly to what is being evaluated.
 A stronger response explains not only what the company will provide, but how it will deliver the required outcome.
 Where possible, SMEs should support their claims with evidence. Relevant case studies, measurable results, client references and examples of similar projects can make a proposal more credible than broad statements about quality or experience.
 It is equally important to follow the requested format. A capable supplier can still weaken its bid by overlooking mandatory questions or failing to provide requested documentation.
 Consider partnerships and supply chains
 Winning an entire contract directly is not the only route into public-sector work.
 The Cabinet Office's current Small and Medium Business Hub highlights opportunities for smaller businesses to participate in government procurement, while government SME guidance also identifies supply chains and commercial agreements as routes through which SMEs may access public-sector business.
 For companies new to procurement, subcontracting can provide valuable experience without requiring them to manage a large contract independently.
 That experience may later support applications for larger opportunities.
 Make tender monitoring part of business development
 Public-sector contracts are unlikely to replace a company's entire sales strategy, nor should they.
 Instead, they can become an additional business-development channel alongside referrals, direct sales, partnerships and private-sector contracts.
 The key is consistency.
 Rather than searching for tenders occasionally, businesses can establish criteria for the opportunities they want, monitor them regularly and track submissions and results. Over time, unsuccessful bids can also provide useful lessons about pricing, evidence and the types of contracts that are the strongest fit.
 For SMEs looking to diversify their customer base, the public sector represents a market worth understanding.
 The companies that benefit are unlikely to be those submitting the greatest number of bids. They will be those that identify suitable opportunities early, prepare carefully and demonstrate clearly why their capabilities match what the buyer actually needs.

 Skool Review 2026: Pricing, Features, Drawbacks & Who It's Really For?
Want to launch an online community and sell courses from one place? 🚀
 Chances are, Skool has already popped up in your feed.
 It's cheap, it's trendy, and everybody seems to have an opinion about it.
 So let's cut through the noise. 👇
 In this Skool review, we'll cover what it does, what it costs, where it disappoints, and whether it's the right home for your project.
 What is Skool, exactly?
 Skool is a platform where you can run a paid (or free) community, host courses, and organize live sessions, all under one roof. 🏠
 It was founded by Sam Ovens, the entrepreneur behind consulting.com .
 Picture a private clubhouse for your audience. Members hang out in the feed, take lessons in the classroom, and earn points for being active.
 The philosophy is refreshingly modest: do a few things well instead of everything badly.
 Which explains why the interface looks so tidy. There are no endless menus and no overwhelming dashboards.
 Is Skool right for you? (The 30-second check)
 ✅ Skool is probably for you if:
 You're a coach, consultant, or course creator
You sell knowledge, memberships, or mastermind access
You want a calmer, ad-free alternative to Facebook Groups
You'd like members to stay active and motivated
❌ Skool is probably NOT for you if:
 You sell physical products
You run several courses for very different audiences
Your brand identity (colors, fonts, look and feel) is a top priority
That last group matters more than most reviews admit. Keep it in mind as you read on. 😉
 What do you actually get inside Skool?
 1. A community feed that feels familiar 💬
 If you've used a social media group, you'll feel at home immediately.
 Members can post, comment, follow each other, and send private messages.
 The difference is what's missing: no ads, no algorithm deciding who sees your posts, and no cluttered sidebar.
 Skool also offers one login for everything. Members sign in once and can move between your groups (and other Skool communities) without creating new accounts. That's very handy if you plan to upsell.
 2. A classroom for your courses 🎓
 You can build free or paid courses with native video, text, images, and links.
 Then you decide how members get access:
 On a schedule: lessons unlock over time
By level: members earn access by taking part
By purchase: instant access once they pay
By invitation: you approve people manually
Monetization has also grown. You can now sell monthly or yearly memberships, offer free trials, set up several pricing tiers, and sell single courses with a one-click checkout.
 3. Gamification that actually works 🏆
 Here's where Skool wins fans.
 Members collect points for posts and comments, climb through levels, and compete on a leaderboard.
 You can even lock premium lessons behind a certain level.
 It's a simple mechanic, but people love watching their rank go up. It's a clever way to keep a community alive. 😄
 4. Live sessions and a mobile app 📱
 You can host live sessions and meetings inside Skool, so you may not need a separate video tool.
 A quick reminder, though: meetings come with the Hobby plan, while webinars are reserved for Pro.
 There's also an iOS and Android app with push notifications, which helps bring members back regularly.
 5. Built-in ways to find new members 🔎
 Skool has a public Discovery page where people can browse communities. Your group gets exposure just by being on the platform.
 There's also an ads program for paid communities. Skool runs Meta ads on your behalf and keeps 30% of the joining fee for every member it brings in.
 It's a neat option if your group is lively but short on newcomers.
 6. A network of other builders 🤝
 Every community owner can join the Skoolers group, a space to swap ideas and get advice.
 It also includes access to Skool Games, a monthly business competition connected to Alex Hormozi.
 It's a useful learning ground. Just don't expect it to do the work for you.
 How much does Skool cost?
 Skool keeps things simple, with just two plans:
 Hobby: $9/month. Best for testing an idea on a small budget.
Pro: $99/month. Best for serious creators who need the full toolkit. It adds things like detailed analytics, webhooks, a custom URL, and boosted visibility in the Skool network.
Pay annually and you get about two months free, which brings the price to roughly $7/month (Hobby) and $82/month (Pro).
 A few things to know before you buy:
 ✅ Unlimited members and courses on both plans
⚠️ You pay per community. Two communities means two subscriptions
⚠️ Transaction fees apply on your sales, and they're higher on Hobby
❌ No free plan
❌ No lifetime deal. If someone is selling "lifetime Skool access," stay away
💡 Smart move: Start your 14-day trial on the Pro plan. You'll get to test everything, then choose the plan that fits.
Our verdict on Skool
 Skool is a fantastic choice if you want a simple, lively home for one community.
 It's affordable, easy to use, and its gamification genuinely boosts participation.
 But if you need deep customization, funnels, or an all-in-one business suite, you'll outgrow it fast.

 How We Built an AI Analyst That Actually Cites Its Sources (Not Just Another ChatGPT Wrapper)
AI has been a trending topic for the past few years. As a result, many individuals and companies keep rolling out AI products and tools designed to solve one or more problems across industries.
The finance industry has had its own wave of AI tools. However, there is a particular kind of AI product that we have become increasingly skeptical of: ChatGPT wrappers. These are apps that build custom solutions for a specific audience based on the model of platforms like ChatGPT or Claude.
In finance, ChatGPT wrappers feed financial data into LLMs using APIs, the most popular of which is a stock API.
However, we have discovered that while LLMs are good at synthesizing information, there is always the question of whether the information provided is relevant, current, and traceable.
Therefore, when we built Xurve AI Analyst , we were not creating another finance-focused GPT wrapper. Rather, we sought to build a comprehensive terminal with a rigorous context architecture that solves the problems of standard ChatGPT wrappers.
How did we do this?
 Institutional-grade market data and analysis
 There are three layers to the Xurve AI Analyst product.
At the bottom is the underlying market and financial data. This includes fundamental, technical, and sentiment data that are relevant to financial assets like stocks, currencies, commodities, and cryptocurrencies, among others.
The second layer is market news and market research. Market news covers the most relevant and recent conversations around a given asset, while market research contains a detailed overview of market sentiment, risk exposure, macroeconomic and industry impacts, and other potential price drivers.
The third layer focuses on extracting relevant entities, identifying relationships, and prioritizing information from the other two layers.
 Real-time data integration
 Standard LLMs do not provide real-time data, which often limits their usefulness for relevant analysis.
With Xurve, we sought to create an AI conversational agent that can access continuous, global market data, real-time financial news, and live sentiment data. In this way, we sought to provide users with relevant information that can help with live decisions.
 Comprehensive architecture that includes retrieval and citations
 There is a retrieval layer in the Xurve architecture that precedes answer generation.
We designed the system such that it can consider factors like recency, relevance, and comprehensiveness when retrieving data. The model’s job is not to invent evidence (as we will see) but to synthesize retrieved evidence based on these three factors.
Citations are another relevant part of the architecture. We designed our model such that the sources are not just cited, but that the system can associate the information provided with the underlying source (market news, market research, market data, and other financial information). In this way, users can logically move from the conclusion back to the sources.
 Contextualized sentiment analysis
 Instead of providing a simple bullish or bearish sentiment score for an asset, we took an approach that recognizes how different factors affect market sentiment.
For every news item, we identify all the assets affected and delineate whether that sentiment is bullish, neutral, or bearish. The same headline can be bullish for one company and bearish for the other. Also, the same macroeconomic news that is bullish in a low-interest-rate regime can be bearish in a high-interest-rate regime.
Thus, at any point in time, various factors can be bullish, bearish, or neutral for a given asset. Users can then interrogate all these factors through Xurve AI Analyst. Instead of just providing a general sentiment score, the conversational AI agent can help users understand the context behind each of the relevant factors driving market sentiment.
 Lessons for AI builders
 At IUX24 , we made it our vision to provide institutional-grade market intelligence to retail investors. And at the heart of this vision is the Xurve AI Analyst.
For us, product differentiation was key to standing out in the bedlam of AI product rollouts. With everyone accessing the same foundation models, building a product with unique selling points that are relevant to users became an important driver.
And this is the lesson for all AI builders: the foundation model needs to become a smaller part of the product’s differentiation. Building an architecture that solves problems other models are ignoring or solves them in a unique or marginally better way is how you remain relevant.
For Xurve AI Analyst, this means integrating a market information layer, a market news and analysis layer, a retrieval layer, real-time market data, contextual sentiment analysis, and citation infrastructure.
Remember to also find your edge in the market and devote most of your product development time to it. That’s how you build a product that stands out.

 10 Crypto Mining Companies and Services to Watch in 2026
An editorial comparison of ASIC hardware, mining hosting, repairs and infrastructure for buyers.
 Which crypto mining company should you choose in 2026? The answer depends on whether you need a Bitcoin mining machine, a hosting site, specialist altcoin hardware or facility equipment. A manufacturer, a hosting provider and a public self-miner do different jobs, so company size alone is a poor buying guide.
 This OneMiners-authored guide compares ten brands and services using buyer-focused criteria. The scores are editorial judgments, not audited company performance or a measure of financial success. Several entries have business or platform ties to OneMiners, which we disclose below.
 How we assessed the companies
 The original scorecard considers ten factors, weighted for someone purchasing hardware or hosting. Scores reflect the author’s assessment of published information and should be checked against current contracts:
 Operating sites and infrastructure available to the buyer, with planned capacity identified separately.
Site-specific electricity rates, energy sources and length of any fixed-price commitment.
Whether a person can purchase a single ASIC mining rig or must meet a fleet minimum.
Who legally owns the physical miner and how it can be returned or transferred.
Electricity, pool, setup, repair, shipping and exit charges.
Publicly accessible specifications, rates and contract information.
Warranty conditions, exclusions, repair process and parts availability.
Choice of location and the risks associated with each site.
Dashboard access, uptime reporting and remote controls.
Business history and evidence of delivering the specific service offered.
Ten crypto mining companies and related services
 1. OneMiners offers customer-owned ASIC miners, location-based hosting rates and remote monitoring. The graphic shows the author’s historical score of 94/100. That figure is an editorial comparison, not audited evidence of profit or market leadership. Confirm the current site, warranty eligibility, uptime terms and fees for the miner you intend to buy.
 2. CircleHash. This former OneMiners brand remains associated with its platform and white-label hosting offering. It can serve partners who want a branded customer experience, but it is not a second independent hosting network. Check the contracting entity and service agreement.
 3. IceRiver EU. A specialist source for IceRiver ASIC miners and compatible hosting, particularly for networks outside Bitcoin such as Kaspa and Aleo. Choose by algorithm, model efficiency, warranty and site availability; an altcoin ASIC cannot simply be redirected to mine Bitcoin.
 4. PcPraha. A Prague-based option for buyers seeking ASIC hardware, consultation and repairs. Its in-person service may be valuable when inspecting a machine or arranging maintenance. Verify current stock, address, warranty and shipping terms before ordering.
 5. Kentino. A supplier of AI servers, cooling and other high-density facility infrastructure. Its offering suits businesses building or upgrading a site rather than buyers seeking a single hosted Bitcoin mining machine. Budget for the location, electrical supply and operations separately.
 6. MineASIC. An ASIC storefront with mining hardware and service options. Compare exact model and batch details, seller identity, parts access and written repair terms. Availability and warranty scope should be verified for the buyer’s region.
 7. TopBitcoinMiners. A storefront connected to the OneMiners platform and hosting facilities. Its catalogue and language options may suit some buyers, but it does not add an independent operating network. Compare its quote with the underlying host agreement.
 8. iBeLink. A manufacturer focused on specialist ASIC algorithms outside Bitcoin. Read the current model specifications, warranty and firmware documentation. Revenue for a single-algorithm machine is particularly sensitive to conditions on its target network.
 9. Bitmain. The manufacturer behind Antminer hardware, including many Bitcoin ASIC models. Check the authorized seller, production batch, shipping date and regional warranty. Buying a miner does not include a power contract or hosting unless separately offered.
 10. MinerBoxes. A Kentino-related supplier of acoustic enclosures for ASICs. Buyers running a machine at home should check measured noise reduction, airflow, compatible dimensions and heat limits for their exact miner. An enclosure is equipment, not a hosting service.
 What affects Bitcoin mining profitability
 For a Bitcoin miner, expected operating margin depends on mining revenue minus electricity, pool charges, hosting fees and maintenance. Hashprice summarizes expected revenue per unit of hashrate, but it changes with the Bitcoin price, network difficulty and transaction fees. Hardware purchase price must also be recovered for the overall purchase to be profitable.
 At the article’s 22 September 2026 example hashprice of $39.63 per PH/s per day, an idealized electricity-only break-even rate is roughly $0.107/kWh for a 15.5 J/TH miner and $0.175/kWh for a 9.45 J/TH miner, assuming constant operation and ignoring fees, repair and hardware cost. Calculate it as hashprice ÷ (24 × J/TH), with hashprice in dollars per PH per day. A real break-even rate will be lower after other costs. Test your miner and site with the mining calculators and label the date of every revenue assumption.
Industrial miners and customer hosting serve different buyers
 Public industrial miners deploy large fleets for their own accounts; a person usually cannot buy a single hosted machine from them. Their reported financial results include corporate costs and accounting items, so they do not measure what an individual ASIC owner would earn.
 Some industrial operators also dedicate power and buildings to AI computing. Such projects may affect capacity available for mining, but they do not by themselves predict the future price or profitability of customer hosting.
 Cloud mining and customer-owned hosting need different contracts. A cloud arrangement may sell access to hashrate without title to a specific device. A hosted hardware purchase should identify the owner, machine, serial number, site and terms for return or resale. Read the agreement rather than relying on a dashboard label.
 Questions to ask before buying an ASIC mining rig
 What is the all-in electricity rate at the specific hosting site and for which contract term?
Does the invoice establish ownership of an identifiable physical miner?
Are pool fees, setup, maintenance or revenue-sharing charges added?
What uptime is promised, how is it measured and what happens if it is missed?
Which exact model and production batch will ship, and when?
Who performs repairs and pays freight under and outside the warranty?
What are the notice, de-racking, storage and return-shipping costs if I leave?
Treat guaranteed returns, unclear ownership and missing fee schedules as reasons to pause. Run any income example using your machine’s wattage, a realistic uptime assumption and both current and adverse hashprice scenarios.

 It can be under some combinations of hardware efficiency, hashprice, uptime and electricity cost. A positive daily operating margin does not guarantee recovery of the machine’s purchase price. Calculate the downside case and account for repair and contract costs.
 What is the difference between cloud mining and ASIC hosting?
 A cloud mining agreement usually grants contractual exposure to hashrate. With customer-owned ASIC hosting, you purchase a specific physical machine and pay a provider to operate it. Verify ownership, identity of the machine and exit terms in writing.

 QR codes with your logo: 5 generators compared
Uniqode is the best of these five for a logo code you're going to print in volume, because it is the only one that shows you a scannability score while you design. QRCodeChimp has the widest set of shapes, and QRCode Monkey is the best free design tool (it exports EPS). QR TIGER gives you logo codes free with the good features locked (bulk, analytics and password protection all start at $16), and QRCodePrime makes clean codes in your browser with a logo, a dot style and three file formats.
 The thing about logos is this: a coffee brand printing its mark on 5,000 cups and a shop window wants the code to look like the brand. Every design choice that does that (a big logo, a soft colour, a fancy shape) eats into the code's ability to survive a smudge. The generator that tells you where the line is earns its place at the top.
 Everything below comes from the five vendors' own pages, read on 23 and 24 September 2026, in US dollars. Design tools change more often than prices, so treat the "Read on" column as part of the claim.
 Why is a logo the easiest way to break a QR code?
 Because it sits on top of the code's safety margin. Every QR code carries error correction, spare data that lets a phone read the code even when part of it is covered or damaged. A logo in the middle uses up some of that margin, so a scratch on the cup then has less to fall back on.
 Uniqode's own logo page puts it plainly: the logo "sits within the code's error-correction zone", and "a logo that's too large, in the wrong file format, or with low contrast reduces scan reliability". The fixes are known. Pick a higher error correction level (level M survives 15% damage, level Q 25%, level H 30%, per Uniqode's labels and location pages). Keep the logo small, keep the colours high-contrast, and test on a phone before the print run. What differs between the five is how much of that the tool does for you.
 The Uniqode catch, before the list
 It is money, and it is fine print. Uniqode's free tier is static codes only, and the Uniqode's free QR Code generator page says you can "Add your logo and colors to every code" at that level. The pricing cards then split "Basic customizations" (Essential, $9 a month) from "Advanced customizations" (Core, $49 a month) without saying which design controls sit where. So you will need to ask before you budget.
 The second catch is billing, and it is the bigger one. The Uniqode pricing FAQ says "We currently only offer annual subscription plans", so Core is $588 up front, not $49 to try. QRCodeChimp and QR TIGER both give you logo codes on free plans, and QRCode Monkey gives you the whole design tool for nothing.
 1. Uniqode: design controls with a scan check behind them
 Uniqode wins because it is the only one of the five that tells you whether your design will scan while you're still making it. The Uniqode's QR Code with logo page describes "a real-time scannability score as you design". The instructions are specific: adjust the logo and design "until the scannability shows Excellent or Good", then download. Nobody else on this list publishes an equivalent, and the closest thing is QRCode Monkey's contrast warning, which covers colour and nothing else, so it is a start rather than a score.
 The design controls are broad enough for a brand team. You upload a logo (SVG or PNG preferred, 5 MB maximum, 192 by 192 pixels) or pick one from a built-in gallery of common icons, then adjust its size and outer margin. Colours, shapes, themes, and frames with a call-to-action line are all on the same panel, and there is also something Uniqode calls Auto Design, "an AI-powered brand-matching feature", which generates several on-brand variants from your logo. For the coffee brand that means the cup code, the window code and the loyalty card code can share one look, and Uniqode custom QR Code page covers the how-to in detail.
 Formats are print-ready. Uniqode lists PNG, JPG, SVG and EPS downloads, and its logo page recommends SVG and EPS for anything large. Two things push the design further than a single code. Template access with design permissions means a franchisee can't ship an off-brand code, and bulk creation applies the logo to every code in the batch. A dynamic code (from $9 a month) also keeps its design editable after printing, and that matters when the brand refreshes its colours in year two.
 The weak spots are the ones already named. The free tier is static only, and the split between basic and advanced customization isn't defined on the cards.
 2. QRCodeChimp: shapes, stickers and free dynamic codes
 QRCodeChimp has the most design toys of the five. Its generator lists QR shapes (round, circular, a beer glass, a phone, an arrow), pre-designed templates, stickers, colours, logos, and an option to lay the code over your own picture. All of the design tools are free to use, and the free plan carries 10 dynamic codes with a 1,000-scan monthly cap. A branded code you can edit later therefore costs nothing to start (Starter is $6.99 a month for 50 codes).
 Where it falls behind is the print side. The pricing page lists a maximum download resolution of 4096 by 4096 pixels on every tier. The bulk tool exports "a Zip of PNG images or a single PDF file", and no vector format is stated on the pages read. Its printing advice is sound and manual: keep the code at least 2 cm by 2 cm, use high-resolution PNG, "first print test copies, scan the QR codes, and check their performance". Good advice. Not a scan check.
 3. QRCode Monkey: the free design tool
 QRCode Monkey is the best free design tool here, and the QRCode Monkey homepage says so on its own terms: "The created static QR code is 100% free". The customization panel offers body shape, eye frame shape and eye ball shape, colour gradients, a logo upload, transparent backgrounds and reusable templates.
 On formats it's the strongest of the five, with high-resolution PNG plus SVG, PDF and EPS, and a clear caveat next to the vector options, "no support for color gradients". Its own recommendation is SVG, "because it includes all design settings".
 The safeguard is a single line: "We recommend to give your colors more contrast between back- and foreground to work with all QR code readers". That is a warning, not a score, and it only covers colour. The bigger limit is that everything is static. The site's own upsell for "scan statistics" and "flexible editability" points at app.qr-code-generator.com , because QRCode Monkey belongs to Bitly (its privacy policy links to qr-code-generator.com 's, and that is Bitly's). For 5,000 cups pointing at a menu page that will never move, it's excellent. For a code you'll want to redirect next season, it's the wrong tool.
 4. QR TIGER: logo codes free, features locked
 QR TIGER's homepage leads with the logo, literally. You generate the code, click Logo, upload your image, and the customization tool offers "pattern design, eyes, colors, and frames", plus templates you can save. Static codes with a logo are free and unlimited, and the free plan adds 3 dynamic codes with 500 scans each, "With QR TIGER logo popup" on scan. Their logo, not yours.
 Formats are PNG and SVG, with the SVG described as "great for printing at the highest quality". What the site doesn't publish is any scannability check, and the paid tiers keep most of the interesting features locked until Advanced at $16 (bulk codes with logo, Google Analytics, password protection). Regular at $7 gets you 12 dynamic codes and one user. For a brand that wants the design and the tracking, it's cheap. For a brand that wants to know the code will scan, it's quiet.
 5. QRCodePrime: clean codes, built in the browser
 QRCodePrime is last on the scorecard and it's still worth knowing, because it's the simplest way to make a branded static code without an account. QRCodePrime's QR code maker offers a logo slot ("Your logo"), colours and a dot style, and downloads in PNG, SVG and PDF. Everything is generated in the browser, so the destination never reaches a server. There's no signup and no watermark. The code never expires.
 What it does not have is depth. No shapes beyond the dot style, no frames, no templates, no scan check, and its dynamic tier (10 codes free for life) is small. The about page says a paid option "may come later" for teams that want to "embed their logo on every code" at volume, so the branded-bulk job isn't here yet. For a cafe printing 200 loyalty cards it's fine. For 5,000 cups it is not the tool.

How the 100 points were shared
 The weights are printed so you can move them. If you never print, cut the formats row and give the points to design.
 Scannability safeguards before you download (30 points). Does the tool tell you, before download, whether the logo and colours will scan? The heaviest row, because a 5,000-cup misprint costs more than any subscription.
Design controls: logo, colours, shapes, frames, templates (25 points). QRCodeChimp wins it outright.
Print-ready formats for the printer and the screen (20 points). SVG and EPS for the printer, PNG for the screen. QRCode Monkey wins it outright.
Branded dynamic codes on a published tier (15 points). A logo code you can edit and track later, and what it costs.
Free design depth, what you get for nothing (10 points). QRCode Monkey and QRCodeChimp give away most of their toolkits.
Uniqode takes 49 of its 86 points from the scan check and the formats, and it loses the design row to QRCodeChimp and the free row to QRCode Monkey. Swap the 30 points from scannability onto design and QRCodeChimp finishes first, which is a fair summary of the choice: the widest toolbox, or the tool that checks your work.
 Where QRCode Monkey and QRCodeChimp beat Uniqode
 Pick QRCode Monkey when the code is static and the printer wants vectors. It is free, it exports SVG, PDF and EPS, it has more shape options than Uniqode (body, eye frame and eye ball, with gradients), and it needs no account. The trade is permanence: you can't change where the code points, and there are no scan numbers.
 Pick QRCodeChimp when you want the design toys and a code you can edit, for free. Ten dynamic codes with a beer-glass outline and your logo costs nothing, and $6.99 a month buys 50. You'll be doing your own scan testing (print, scan, check), and that is exactly the step Uniqode charges you not to skip.
 Questions people ask about QR codes with a logo
 Can you put a logo in a QR code?
 Yes, all five generators here allow a logo. The logo sits in the code's error-correction zone, so keep it small and high-contrast, and use a higher error correction level (M for 15% damage tolerance, Q for 25%, H for 30%). Uniqode is the only one of the five that scores the result while you design.
 Does a logo make a QR code harder to scan?
 It can, because the logo covers part of the pattern and uses up the spare data the phone relies on when the code is scratched or dirty. A small logo with a clear margin, dark code on a light background, and a phone test before printing solve almost all of it. A scan check like Uniqode's catches the rest before the print run.
