 Four things I keep off my pricing page until ten people have paid
Confession: the first pricing pages I made were mostly costume. Three tiers, a "most popular" badge on the middle one, a big comparison grid, an enterprise box. For a product nobody had paid for yet.
 Nothing on that page was a lie exactly. It just wasn't true yet either, and people can smell that. So now I have a rule for anything new: until ten people have actually paid, these four things stay off the pricing page.
 A "most popular" badge. Popular with who? If zero or two people bought, the badge is a guess dressed up as social proof. I'd rather write one plain line under the plan, like "this is the one I'd pick if I was you, because X".
The "contact us" enterprise tier. It looks grown up, but at this stage it mostly invites discount asks and custom builds a solo founder can't support. One paid plan and a free option is enough to learn if anyone pays at all.
A countdown or "launch price ends Friday". Fake urgency on a page with no buyers reads like a scam to the exact careful people I want early. If the price is going up, I just say the month and why.
A 25 row feature grid. Early on most rows are things I'm still figuring out, and the grid makes people compare checkboxes instead of asking "does this do my one job". Three or four lines in plain words beat it.
What stays on the page: the price, what you get, what you don't get yet, how to cancel in one sentence, and my name. That's it.
 A worked example so this isn't just vibes. Say you're selling a small $12/mo tool that cleans up messy CSV exports. The costume version has Starter / Pro / Team, Pro marked popular, and a grid listing "API access (coming soon)". The honest version says: $12 a month, cleans up to 500 files a month, no API yet, cancel from settings in two clicks, questions go to my email. The second one looks smaller. It's also the one where a stranger can decide in ten seconds without wondering what's hidden.
 After ten payers you actually have data. Which plan they picked, what they asked before buying, who wanted a team seat. Then the badge can be real and the extra tier can be built around a real request instead of a template.
 Picking the number is a harder problem. I wrote up how I think about it in how to price your first SaaS when you have almost no data on the Aura++ blog, the short version is pick a number you can explain out loud and change it later with a note to existing users.
 I know some people will say three tiers is basic anchoring and it works. Maybe it does once you have traffic. So I'm curious: what's on your pricing page right now that you'd quietly delete if nobody was watching?

 Three things I stopped counting as marketing time
For a while my weekly "marketing hours" looked great on paper. Then I actually wrote down what I did in those hours and a big chunk of it was me feeling productive near marketing, not doing marketing.
 So now I have a rule: an hour only counts as marketing if a stranger could see the result, or if it ended with someone new knowing the product exists. Everything else goes in a different bucket, even if it feels like marketing. These are the three that got kicked out first.
 Tweaking the landing page with zero new visitors. Changing the hero line for the fourth time when 30 people saw the last version is not marketing, it's building. Sometimes it's the right thing to do, fine, but I log it as build time. Otherwise I tell myself I "did marketing" all week and nobody new came.
Reading other founders' growth threads. This one hurt. Some of it is useful, but most of it is just comfortable. If I read for an hour and didn't change anything I'm doing this week, that was entertainment. Now it counts as nothing. Not build, not marketing.
Setting up tools. Scheduling apps, analytics dashboards, a new UTM sheet, picking the perfect email tool. All of it feels like progress. None of it puts the product in front of anyone. I give it one block a month and log it as admin.
What's left in the marketing bucket is kind of boring. Replying to people in threads where my kind of user already hangs out. Writing one honest post. Sending a couple of personal emails to people who might actually care. Submitting to a launch site and then being there on the day. Asking a happy user if I can quote them.
 The weird result is my marketing hours went down after I started doing this, and the stuff that actually brings people in went up, simply because I can see it now. When the number was inflated I never felt the need to protect the real hours.
 The test I use at the end of the week is simple. For every hour I called marketing, can I point at a thing someone outside my laptop saw? If not, it moves.
 I'm not saying never polish the landing page or set up tools. Just don't let them hide inside the marketing number. If you want a starting point for how to split the week depending on your stage, there's a longer write-up on how much time a solo founder should spend on marketing vs building on the Aura++ blog, including a small two-week time audit you can copy.
 Curious what's on your list. What's something you used to count as marketing that you quietly don't anymore?

 My dashboard's empty state was the first screen every new user saw, and it said nothing
For a long time the first thing a new user saw after signing up was a grey box that said "No projects yet" and a button that said "New project". That's it. I'd spent weeks on the landing page and maybe ten minutes on the screen people actually land on.
 The problem is that empty screen is where trust gets decided. They just gave you an email, maybe a card. Now they're alone with a blank page and they have to guess what "done" looks like. A lot of people don't guess. They close the tab and tell themselves they'll come back, and they don't.
 So I rewrote it, and these are the four things I check now on any empty state before I call onboarding finished:
 Show a finished example, not a blank. A greyed out sample of what a real filled-in project looks like, clearly labeled as an example. People copy what they can see much faster than they follow instructions.
One action, in their words. Not "Create resource". Whatever the user would say out loud, like "Add your first product". If there are three buttons on an empty screen, there's really zero.
Say how long it takes. "Takes about 2 minutes" next to the button. Sounds silly, but an unknown amount of work is exactly what makes people postpone it.
Tell them what you'll do with their stuff. If the first step asks for a URL, an API key or a file, one plain line about what happens to it. Nobody reads the privacy page at this moment, but they do read one sentence right next to the input.
What I don't put there anymore: a product tour with seven tooltips, a welcome video, a "complete your profile" bar at 10%. All of those are me talking. The empty state should be the user doing the first thing.
 The way I test it is boring. I make a fresh account in a private window, don't read anything I wrote, and see if I know what to do in five seconds. Then I ask one person who has never seen the product to do the same while I keep my mouth shut. The second test is the one that hurts, and the useful one.
 I wrote longer about the emails side of this, the ones that go out after the first screen, in this guide on onboarding emails that get people to a first win . But honestly fix the empty screen first. An email can't rescue someone who already decided your app is confusing.
 Curious what yours shows right now. If you open your own app in a private window today, what's the first thing a stranger sees?

 Two weeks after a launch I ask my three happiest users for a review, on one site only
Launch week gives you a bunch of nice comments that nobody outside that thread will ever see. Two weeks later the comments are buried and the people who wrote them are just... users. So around day 14 I do one small thing, and I keep it small on purpose.
 I pick three people. Not the loudest ones from launch day, the ones who actually came back. Logged in more than once in week two, used the thing for real work, maybe replied to an email. If I can't find three people like that, that's my answer, and I go fix onboarding instead of collecting reviews.
 Then I pick one review site. Just one. For a B2B tool that's usually whichever one buyers in that niche already check, G2 or Capterra most of the time. Sending three people to five different sites gets you one review on each and a profile that looks dead everywhere. Three reviews in one place at least looks like a product somebody uses.
 The ask is a plain email, written by me, sent one at a time:
 one line on what they did with the product (so they know it's not a blast)
the direct link to the review form, not the profile page
"two or three sentences is plenty, say what you used it for"
no discount, no gift card, nothing in exchange. The review sites have rules about incentives and honestly a bribed review reads like one anyway
If someone says no or ignores it, that's fine, I don't follow up more than once. And if one of them writes back with a complaint instead of a review, that's the more useful email of the two.
 Why day 14 and not launch day: on launch day people are reacting to the idea. Two weeks in they're reacting to the product, and that's what a buyer reading reviews wants to hear about.
 The Aura++ Studio has a longer write-up on getting your first G2 and Capterra reviews if you want the setup side (claiming the profile, categories, all that).
 Curious how others do this. Do you ask right after launch while people are excited, or wait until they've actually used it for a while?

 Five days after a launch I stop counting signups and count who finished the first real task
Launch week always gives me a signup number I like looking at. By day five that number has stopped meaning much. Some of those people opened the app once, saw an empty screen and left. They are in the total anyway, and the total makes me feel like the launch worked.
 So on day five I do a boring thing. I pick one action that means someone actually used the product. Not "verified email", not "visited dashboard". The first real task. For a scheduler that's the first post scheduled, for an invoicing tool it's the first invoice sent. One action, written down before I look at any data so I can't pick the one that flatters me.
 Then I go through the launch cohort by hand. Everyone who signed up during launch week, one row each, and a yes or no for that task. If they didn't do it I write the last screen they reached. With launch-size numbers this takes maybe 30 minutes and its way more useful than any chart.
 The pattern is usually obvious once its in rows. Most people who quit, quit on the same screen. That screen is my week. Not a new feature, not another launch board.
 My rule now: if less than half the launch signups finished the first task, I don't launch anywhere else until I've fixed that screen. Sending more strangers into the same hole just makes the signup number bigger and the problem the same.
 The people who got stuck get one short email from me, asking what they expected to happen on that screen. Not a drip, one email. I wrote up how I think about that first-win email in this guide on onboarding emails that get people to their first win if you want the longer version.
 What's the one action you count as "actually used it" for your product? Curious how many of you picked it before looking at the numbers.

 After yesterday's traffic spike I stopped celebrating Direct visits and counted Google instead
Yesterday Aura++ showed 1,584 DataFast visits by about 7:40pm IST. That number looks great in a screenshot. It is also mostly junk. 1,466 of those visits were Direct/None. Bounce sat at 93.37%. 1,526 were new. I am not celebrating the headline.
 Google was 87. That is the number I actually kept. Direct spikes like that usually mean bots, prefetch, or people who bounced before they read a word. Treating 1,584 as "organic growth from posting" would be lying to myself. So the rule I am sticking with: separate Direct junk from Google before you tell anyone a traffic story.
 Sales that day were clearer than the traffic story. Polar showed two Aura++ Premium launches with paid net above zero — $29.00 and $17.40, so $46.40 total. Five other paid-status orders were $0 with a full discount. Those do not count as revenue. I wrote both lines down the same way I write launch checklists: the real number, not the rounded story.
 I run Aura++ as a daily launch board, so I see this trap a lot. A maker posts "we hit 1.5k visitors" and then wonders why nobody signed up. If bounce is that high and almost everything is Direct, the room was never full of buyers. It was full of noise.
 What I do now after any spike: open the referrer breakout first, ignore Direct until I have a reason to trust it, and only then compare Google day over day. If Google barely moved, the spike did not teach me anything about distribution. If Google moved and bounce on that slice is sane, then I have something worth iterating on.
 Curious how you split Direct vs search when your analytics look like a win at first glance. Do you throw Direct out by default, or dig before you decide?

 This morning I shipped live launch stats for makers, because empty rooms stay empty when nobody can see the visitors
For a while I kept watching the same pattern on Aura++. A maker launches, the listing looks fine, and then the room stays quiet. Not because nobody visited. Because the maker had no way to see that anyone visited. No visitors today, no outbound clicks, nothing to screenshot and send to their own audience. So they don't bring more people, and the empty room stays empty.
 This morning I shipped a fix for that. Every project now has a shareable live stats page (the /live URL under the project). It shows recorded visitors today and this week, verified upvotes, guest upvotes, outbound clicks to the website, GitHub and X, and comments. The project page links to "Live stats". The card has copy link, Share on X, and Share on LinkedIn.
 Example that I checked live: the Kai live stats page on Aura++ . Same shape works on a couple other live projects I spot-checked too. Homepage still has no "Live now" block on purpose. This is for the maker, not a global ticker.
 I also tightened guest upvotes while I was in there. Max three guest votes per project total, pending plus claimed. No IP limit. Browser rate limit unchanged. Winners still count signed-in votes only. After a guest vote, Google sign-in is still prompted, so the guest path is a nudge, not a loophole.
 The thing I care about isn't the page itself. It's that a maker can finally open something during launch day and say "we got X visitors and Y clicks" without waiting for me to email a digest later. If they can see value, they bring their people. If they can't, they refresh the listing once and go back to work.
 Curious if you'd share a live stats link mid-launch, or if you'd rather keep the numbers private until the week ends.

 The week after a launch I search for everyone who mentioned my product without tagging me
During launch week the notifications do all the work. People tag you, comment on the listing, reply to your post. Then it goes quiet and it's easy to think that was all the talk there was.
 It usually isn't. A lot of people who talk about a new product never tag the maker. They mention it in a reply to someone else, in a "stuff I tried this week" thread, in a newsletter roundup, or with the name spelled a bit wrong.
 So around day seven after a launch I block one hour and search for the product by hand. Not alerts, by hand, because alerts miss the misspellings and the screenshots.
 What I search, every time:
 the product name in quotes on X, Reddit and here on IH
the domain without the https, since people paste it bare
the two most likely misspellings (for mine that's stuff like "aura plus plus" or "auraplus")
the name plus the word "alternative"
For every hit I write three things in a sheet: where it was, what they called the product in their own words, and if somebody asked a question there that nobody answered.
 Then two things happen. If there's an unanswered question I reply to it, even a week late. In my experience nobody minds a useful answer that shows up late. And the words people used go into a list, because how a stranger describes your product is very often better copy than what's on your landing page.
 The "alternative" search is the one I'd keep if I could only do one. It shows which product people put you next to, and that's rarely the one you'd guess from your own positioning.
 I do this after every Aura++ launch now. It's also how I decide which channels to keep, the same way I wrote about in keeping only the three channels that sent a stranger who replied . An untagged mention from a stranger counts.
 Do you go looking for the untagged mentions after a launch, or mostly trust the notifications?

 This morning I ran my own launch site through the AI recall check I just shipped, and it got 0 out of 40
Today I shipped a small free thing on Aura++, an AI Visibility Score. You paste a URL and it gives a score out of 100. The biggest chunk, 40 points, is AI recall: the tool actually asks a model three questions, what is this product, what are the best tools in its category, and what are the alternatives to it. The rest is crawlability for AI bots (GPTBot, ClaudeBot, PerplexityBot), on-page clarity, and off-site footprint. You get a grade A to F and three fixes.
 Obviously the first URL I tested was my own. EarlyHunt, one of my launch sites.
 It scored 53/100, grade F. And the split is the part that annoyed me:
 Crawlability for AI: 20/20. All three bots allowed.
On-page clarity: 20/20. Title, meta description, H1, all there.
AI recall: 0/40. Zero on all three questions.
When it asked "What is EarlyHunt?" the model said: "I do not have information about a product called EarlyHunt." Then for best launch platforms it listed Product Hunt, BetaList, AngelList, Crunchbase... and LaunchDarkly, which is a feature flag tool. So the model will happily put a feature flag company in my category, but not me.
 That's the takeaway I keep coming back to. Green technical SEO only means bots can read your page. It doesn't mean anyone wrote about you enough for a model to remember you. The gap is off-site, and I had been skipping that work because the checklist felt done.
 The three fixes it gave were boring and correct: a quotable about page that gets cited elsewhere, a comparison page that names the product inside its category, and a proper alternatives page. Those are my next week.
 I wrote the longer version, with my other site's answers word for word, on Medium: my launch site scored 0/40 on AI recall while its SEO checks were all green . If you want to see where yours lands, the free AI Visibility Score on Aura++ takes about 10 seconds, no signup, 5 checks a day. It's v1, so if your score looks wrong tell me, I'd rather know.
 Has an AI assistant ever named your product in an alternatives answer? What got you there?

 Three weeks after a launch I only keep the three channels that sent a stranger who replied
Launch week is noisy. Every board, every Discord, every "share this" thread looks like it worked because the graph goes up. Three weeks later the graph is flat and I still have 12 channels bookmarked that I somehow promised myself I'd keep posting on.
 So I changed the filter. I don't count clicks. I don't even count signups anymore for this. I count stranger replies. Email, comment, DM, whatever, from someone I don't already know, who said something about the product. If a channel never produced one of those in three weeks, it gets cut, even if it sent traffic.
 I keep a dumb sheet. Columns are channel, date I posted, traffic that week (rough), and then a column for stranger replies with a short note of what they said. After three weeks I sort by that last column. Usually three channels have something real. The rest have zeros and a story I was telling myself.
 Rule I stick to now:
 keep at most three channels that actually sent a stranger who replied
drop anything with zero replies after three weeks, even if it had a spike on day one
don't add a new board until one of those three is clearly working again, not just "feels busy"
This cut my weekly posting load in half and weirdly didn't hurt signups much. The quiet channels were mostly vanity. The ones that stayed were usually one niche community, one weekly launch page, and one place where people already talk about the problem.
 I do this for launches around Aura++ . Earlier I also wrote about checking Search Console after the spike , which is the other half of the same habit. Referrers lie. Replies don't.
 How do you decide which channels to keep after the launch week ends?
