 What an AI phone assistant should (and shouldn't) do on a small business's calls
When we launched AI Agent Studio here two weeks ago, we said our biggest open question was trust. Every business owner considering an AI phone assistant has the same worry: "I like the idea, but what if it says something stupid to a customer?"
 That's the right worry. An AI phone assistant that answers every call but handles them badly can do more damage than a voicemail box. So this post isn't about whether to use one. It's about where to draw the lines if you do.
 It applies to any AI voice tool, not just ours.
 Why small businesses look at this at all
 For a clinic, an estate agent, or an online shop, the phone rings at the worst times: during appointments, at lunch, after closing, on weekends. Many callers who reach voicemail don't leave a message. They call the next business on the list.
 An AI phone assistant answers every call, any time, and handles the routine ones. The question is what counts as routine.
 What the agent should handle
 These are calls with a clear answer that lives in your documents or systems:
 Opening hours, location, parking and directions
Questions answered by your own documents: prices, services, what to bring, policies. The agent should answer from what you've uploaded, not from general knowledge.
Booking, moving, and cancelling appointments, if it's connected to your calendar or booking system
Order status, if it's connected to your store
Taking a detailed message: name, number, reason for calling, and how urgent it is. A structured message is already better than most voicemails.
Simple qualification: for an estate agent, "buying or renting, which area, what budget" before a person calls back
What it should hand to a person
 The agent should transfer the call, or take a message and promise a callback, when:
 The caller is upset. One calm attempt to help, then a person.
Money is in dispute: refunds outside the normal policy, double charges, billing errors
The caller asks for a human. Always, and immediately. Don't make them ask twice.
The answer isn't in its documents. "Let me get someone who can answer that" is far better than a confident guess.
There's a sale worth a person's time: a large order, a high-value property enquiry
What it should never do
 Write these into the agent's rules on day one:
 Never pretend to be human. Say it's an AI assistant at the start of the call. Most callers don't mind; they mind being fooled.
Never give medical, legal, or financial advice. For clinics: anything urgent gets directed to emergency services or a clinician, with no attempt to assess it.
Never take card numbers by voice. Send a secure payment link instead.
Never promise what a person hasn't approved: discounts, exceptions, delivery dates it can't check
Never record calls without telling the caller, and check the consent rules where you operate
Setting it up in an afternoon
 Here's the order we'd suggest for a first agent:
 1. List your top 10 call reasons. Look at your call log, voicemails, and the questions staff answers most. These become the agent's job description.
 2. Upload what it needs to know. Hours, price list, service descriptions, FAQs, policies. If something isn't written down, the agent can't answer it, and that's a useful discovery in itself.
 3. Write three lists: handles, hands off, never. Use the lists above as a starting point.
 4. Test it with 20 calls from your own team. Have people play awkward callers: the angry one, the one with a strange accent, the one who asks for a person three times. Fix what goes wrong.
 5. Start with after-hours only. Let it answer calls you would have missed anyway. If it makes a mistake, it's on a call that would have gone to voicemail.
 6. Read the transcripts every week. Look for questions it couldn't answer (add them to your documents) and transfers that weren't needed (tighten the rules).
 What to measure
 Keep it simple. Track four numbers each week:
 Calls answered that would have been missed
Calls handled without a transfer
Calls transferred, and why
Complaints about the AI
If the first goes up and the last stays at zero, widen its hours.
 Where AI Agent Studio fits
 In AI Agent Studio, you can give any agent its own phone number. It answers in a natural voice 24/7, answers from the documents you upload (with sources you can check), and can act in more than 100 connected apps, for example, creating a contact or updating a ticket after the call. You describe its role, rules, and approvals in plain English; there's no code. When it needs a person, it transfers the call.
 Starter is $79/month, and Growth is $399/month, with a free trial, so you can run 20 test calls before you pay.

As a caller, does hearing "I'm an AI assistant" make you more or less likely to stay on the line?
What's the one rule you'd add to the "never" list?

 Launching AI Agent Studio: no-code AI agents that act on your business systems, not just chat
Most "AI agents" sold to small businesses are chatbots with a new name. They answer questions, and then a person still has to update the ticket, book the call, or send the follow-up.
 We wanted agents that do those steps themselves. That's AI Agent Studio ( aiagents.dev ), which we're launching here today.
 What it does
 - Voice agents: connect a phone number, and an agent answers calls in a natural voice, 24/7
 - Database Q&A: ask questions of a live database in plain English, with no SQL
 - Document search: upload manuals and SOPs and get answers with source references
 - Actions in 100+ apps: create contacts, send messages, update tickets
 - Multi-step workflows: chain agents with triggers and approvals across email, SMS, voice, APIs, and webhooks
 - No code: you build it in plain language
 Who it's for
 Small and mid-sized businesses that want to automate support, approvals, calls, and operations without hiring developers.
 Pricing
 Starter is $79/month, Growth is $399/month, and Enterprise is custom, with white-labelling. There's a free trial.
 What we're unsure about
 1. Trust. Letting an agent act inside your CRM or on a customer call is a big step. We've built approvals in, but we don't know yet where most businesses want to draw the line.
 2. The starting price. Is $79/month right for a small team trying its first agent?
 3. What to lead with. Voice, database Q&A, and workflows are three different pitches, and we're not sure which one to put first.
 Questions for you
 1. Which business task would you hand to an AI agent first?
 2. What would stop you from letting an agent act in your systems?
 3. Looking at the landing page, which feature would make you try it?
