 Three questions I'd ask before letting someone into early access, and none of them is about their company
When I first ran an early-access list, the form asked for company name, company size and role. Then I let people in roughly in signup order. Looking back, none of those fields told me who would actually use the thing and tell me what was wrong with it, which is the whole point of early access.
 If I set up the form again, these are the three questions I'd ask instead.
 1. "What are you using for this right now?" A specific answer, like a spreadsheet, a competitor, or a script they wrote, means the problem is real for them today. "Nothing yet" or a blank usually means curiosity. Curious people are nice, but they don't log in on day three.
 2. "When would you try it if you got in this week?" Free text, not a dropdown. "Monday, I have a launch on the 10th" is a very different person from "whenever". I'd let the Monday person in first, every time.
 3. "Can I message you if something breaks or I have a question?" A yes here is basically a yes to giving feedback. If someone says no, that's fine, they can wait for the public version. The early batch should be people who are okay with a slightly broken product and a founder in their inbox.
 The company questions can come later, once they're in and it actually matters for setup.
 Small batch, too. I'd rather let in ten people who answered all three well than fifty who signed up first. Ten people you actually talk to gives you more than fifty who never open the welcome email.
 EarlyHunt's Studio has a longer write-up on deciding who gets into early access , including what to do with the people you don't pick yet.
 How do you pick your early users? Signup order, gut feeling, or something on the form?

 I fixed my backlink sheet on Oct 20. By Oct 29 it was wrong again, so every row has a date now
Small opinion that I've come round to the hard way: a backlink isn't a fact, it's a state. It was true on the day you checked it. That's all you know.
 Here's what changed my mind. Our sheet said our SaaSHub link was nofollow. On Oct 20 I actually checked, and the "Visit website" link was followed. I fixed the sheet and felt good about it. On Oct 29 I checked again and it was rel=nofollow again. Same listing, same page. Meanwhile our PeerPush "Visit site" link only has rel=noopener, so it's still followed. If I'd trusted the sheet either time, I'd have been wrong about one of them.
 So the decision I made: the sheet doesn't get a "dofollow: yes/no" column anymore. Every link gets a status and a checked-on date. A row without a date is a guess.
 And the check itself takes about two minutes a listing:
 View Source on the live listing page, find your link, read the rel value
Look for a robots meta tag or header that says noindex or nofollow for the whole page
Check the canonical, in case the page points Google somewhere else
Confirm the link is still there at all. Sometimes it just isn't
Disclosure since it's relevant: I run launch sites too, and on Aura++ the $29 premium listing is the dofollow one, plus a $5 founder profile upgrade. So I'm not neutral about directories. I just don't think anyone, us included, should be trusted on rel values without a recent check. If you don't feel like reading HTML, the Aura++ dofollow link checker does it for you.
 I wrote the longer version, with the full audit, on Medium: why your backlink spreadsheet is probably wrong by now .
 How often do you recheck links you've already logged? Or is it a one-time thing once the launch is done?

 Three things I tell closed beta users before the free part ends, and the price is the first one
The worst way to end a free beta is an email on a Monday that says "thanks for helping, it's $29 a month from Friday". The product didn't change. The deal did, and nobody was told it was coming. That's what makes it feel like a bait and switch, not the price itself.
 So now I tell beta users three things, early and plainly.
 1. The price, in the first email, even if it's a guess. "This will probably be around $19 a month when it's out of beta" is way better than silence. People can decide if they're the kind of user who'll pay that, and the ones who aren't stop expecting free forever. If the number changes later, I just say it changed and why.
 2. The end date, or the condition that ends it. Either "free until March 1" or "free until the core workflow is stable and onboarding works without me on a call". Then about two weeks before it ends, a personal note, not a banner. A countdown bar in the app reads like pressure. A short email from the founder reads like a heads up.
 3. What happens if they don't pay. Can they export their data? Do they keep read only access for a bit? Can they still send feedback? Saying "you won't lose anything you made here" removes most of the fear, and honestly it costs almost nothing to offer.
 The people I'd ask first aren't the ones who said nice things in a call. It's the ones who used the main feature more than once in the last two weeks, invited a teammate, or emailed about a missing export because a real job depended on it. That's buying behaviour.

 If you've run a closed beta, did you say the price upfront, or wait until the end? How did that go?

 I cap design partners at five, and every one of them hears the price before the first call
When I start something B2B, I want a few real companies using it while I build. Design partners, basically. The trap I keep seeing is that "design partner" quietly turns into "free user who gives feedback when they feel like it". So I keep two numbers fixed.
 Five, max. Every partner needs real founder time: a kickoff where I watch how they work today, regular calls, follow ups on what they asked for. As a solo founder I can't do that properly for more than five. Common Paper says most startups start with five or fewer, and a16z warns against going past 20 just because inbound looks good. Past five I stop learning and start doing support.
 A price, said out loud before we start. Free during the program is fine, a small paid pilot is fine, free now and a written discount later is fine. What isn't fine is "we'll figure out pricing later". If the price after the program isn't in the agreement, the last call becomes an awkward negotiation instead of a yes or no. And around halfway I ask straight up: "at the price we agreed, would you keep it?" A hesitant yes tells me a lot.
 The price also filters people. Someone who says "sure, I'd pay that if it solves X" behaves differently on calls than someone who just likes trying new tools. They show up, they bring the messy real cases, they tell me when something is useless.
 One thing I track is how many partners hit the same problem. One partner asking for something is a request. Three of five asking is the roadmap. That count stops me from building the product for whoever talks the loudest.
 And if none of the five would pay at the end, I try not to explain it away. It usually means the problem isn't painful enough, I picked the wrong kind of company, or the product isn't there yet. All three are cheaper to learn with five companies than after a launch.
 We just put out a longer guide to running a design partner program before your product exists , with an agreement outline and a 30-day plan, if you want something to copy.
 If you've run design partners, did you charge them anything? Did it change who stuck around?

 Three things that have to be true before I ask an early user for an intro
A warm intro from an early user is probably the best lead you can get at the start. Someone who uses your thing tells a friend "this helped me", and the friend actually listens. But asking too early makes it awkward for both of you, so I wait until three things are true.
 1. They've used it for real at least three times. Not signed up, not clicked around once. Three sessions where they did the thing the product is for. Before that they don't have a story to tell, and I'd be asking them to vouch for something they haven't really tried.
 2. They said something nice without me asking. A reply like "this saved me an hour" or "my cofounder liked it". If I have to fish for the compliment first, the intro would be a favour, not a recommendation, and the other person can feel the difference.
 3. I've fixed at least one thing they reported. This one is underrated imo. Once someone has seen you listen and actually ship the fix, they trust you with their friends. Before that you're a stranger with a beta.
 When all three are true the ask is small and specific. Something like: "You mentioned the export thing last week. Is there one person you know with the same problem? I can send you a two-line blurb to forward, and no worries at all if nobody comes to mind." One person, not "anyone you know". A blurb they can forward, so it costs them 30 seconds. And a real way out.
 Then I follow up once, max. If they forget, that's fine. They're still a user, and that matters more than the intro.
 And if someone does intro you, tell them how it went, even if it's just "we talked, not a fit yet, thanks a lot". People who see their intro went somewhere tend to make another one.
 If you want something to copy from, the EarlyHunt guide on asking early customers for warm introductions has email templates, forwardable blurbs and a simple sheet to track every ask.
 Have you asked an early user for an intro yet? What made it feel like the right time, or did it just feel weird?

 Four things I don't promise early-access users, even when it would get them to sign up
When you're trying to get your first 20 or 30 people into early access, it's really tempting to promise stuff. Someone is on the fence, and one sentence from you would get them in. I've said some of these sentences before and regretted them later, so now there's a short list I keep out of the invite emails and the calls.
 "Free forever." Early users getting it free for a while is fine. Forever is a long time, and you don't know yet what a user costs you. What I say instead: free during early access, and I'll tell you the price at least a few weeks before it ends.
 A founding price "for life" with no details. I like giving early people a better price, they took the risk. But "for life" without saying which plan, which limits and what happens if they downgrade is a future argument. If I offer it, I write it down: this price, this plan, as long as you stay subscribed. One line, in the email, so we both have it.
 A date for a feature they asked for. "That's coming in two weeks" gets a yes today and an annoyed email in three weeks. I say "it's on the list, here is why it's not next" or I don't mention it at all. If it ships, they hear about it first.
 Building something just for them. If one person needs a custom thing to sign up, they're probably not the user I'm building for yet. I'd rather lose that signup than spend a week on a feature only they use, then maintain it for months.
 The funny thing is people seem fine with the honest version. "No dates, but I read every message and the price won't surprise you" sounds less exciting, but nobody has to be disappointed later. And early-access people talk to each other, so one broken promise gets around fast.
 The bit about telling people the price early and moving them from free to paid without burning trust is covered pretty well in how to convert closed beta users into paying customers on the EarlyHunt blog.
 What's a promise you made to an early user that came back to bite you? Mine was a date. It's always a date.

 My early-access welcome email had six links in it. Now it has one
Small confession about my own onboarding. The first welcome email I wrote for early-access people had six links. Docs, a getting-started video, the changelog, a feedback form, a community link and finally the app itself, near the bottom.
 I thought that was being helpful. Looking back it was me being nervous and throwing everything at them, hoping something sticks. If you give someone six things to click, the most likely outcome is they click nothing and tell themselves they'll come back later. Later doesn't come.
 The rule I use now: an early-access welcome email gets one link, and it goes straight to the one action that shows the product working. Not the dashboard. The action.
 How I pick that one action, roughly:
 what's the first thing a user does that makes them go "oh, ok, that's useful"? For a scheduling tool it's sending one real booking link, for an analytics tool it's seeing their own first chart
can I deep link to it, with as little setup in front of it as possible? Ideally the link lands them on that screen with an example already filled in
can they do it in under five minutes? If not, the action is too big and I split it
Worked example for a made-up invoice tool. Old email: welcome, here's the docs, here's the video, join the community, give feedback, open the app. New email:
 "You're in. The one thing worth doing first is sending yourself a test invoice, it takes about two minutes and you'll see exactly what your customer gets. [Send a test invoice]. If anything is confusing, just hit reply, it comes to me."
 That's it. The reply-to is the feedback form. The docs and changelog go into a second email two or three days later, and only for people who actually did the first action. People who didn't get a short nudge about that same one action instead, not a new list of things.
 What I'd avoid:
 a "getting started" checklist with eight steps on day one
linking to a community before there's anyone in it
asking for feedback before they've used anything, there's nothing to give feedback on yet
There's a good piece on the same problem inside the app, when people land on an empty screen and don't know what to type, in how to get new users past the blank prompt box on the EarlyHunt blog.
 For those running early access right now: how many links are in your welcome email? And do you know which one people actually click?

 I tell early-access people the real batch size, even when the number is small
A lot of early-access pages say "limited spots" and never say how limited. I get the instinct, a small number feels a bit embarrassing. But I've stopped hiding it. My rule now is that the invite email and the waitlist page both say the actual batch size, how many people are still waiting, and roughly when the next batch opens.
 Why I think the real number works better than vague scarcity:
 "limited spots" with no number reads like marketing, and people on waitlists have seen it a hundred times. A real number like 15 reads like an actual person deciding how many people he can support properly.
it sets the expectation of attention. If you're one of 15, you expect the founder to reply to you. That's true, and it's the main thing early users get that later users won't.
the people who didn't get in stop guessing. If they know there are 120 ahead of them and batches go out every few weeks, they can decide to wait or leave. Silence makes them assume the product is dead.
Worked example, numbers made up to show the shape. Say the waitlist has 140 people and batch two is 18 seats. The invite email to the 18 says something like:
 "You're one of 18 people in batch two. There are about 120 still on the list. I picked this batch because you all said you do X weekly, which is the thing I want to get right first. I'll reply to everything myself, so please do tell me what breaks."
 And the 120 who didn't get in get a short note too:
 "Batch two went out today, 18 people. You're still on the list. Next batch is planned for roughly three weeks from now, and it'll be bigger if this one goes ok. If you want out, reply and I'll remove you."
 That second email is the one most of us skip, and it's the one that keeps the list from going cold.
 What I'd avoid:
 a fake counter that ticks down "spots left" when there's no real limit
a public "1,000 people waiting" number you can't back up later
a specific date for the next batch you aren't sure of. "Roughly three weeks" is fine, a date you then miss is worse than no date
The odd side effect is it forces you to actually decide the batch size before you send, instead of inviting whoever and hoping. Picking who goes in which batch is its own thing, and there's a decent rubric for it in how to decide who gets into early access and who should wait on the EarlyHunt blog.
 Genuine question for people who've run a waitlist: did you ever show the real numbers? Did it make people more patient, or less?

 No Discord for my early-access users until someone asks for one twice
Mildly unpopular take: an early-access product doesn't need a Discord on day one. A lot of early-access servers end up looking the same: a #general with the founder's welcome message, two "hey" replies, and then silence. That silence is visible to every new person who joins, and it says "nobody's here" louder than any landing page can say otherwise.
 So my rule is simple. No community server for early users until at least two different people ask for a place to talk to each other. Not to me, to each other. That's the actual job a community does.
 Until then, what I'd use instead:
 plain email, one to one. Early users tell you more in a private reply than in a public channel, because they don't have to look smart in front of strangers.
a short update every week or two, sent to everyone, with what shipped, what broke, and one question. People can reply to it, and the replies come to you, not into an empty room.
a public changelog page so new batch members can see the thing is moving without needing to scroll a dead channel.
Worked example. Say batch one is 25 people. You set up a Discord with 6 channels. Maybe 15 join, 4 post once, and by week three the last message in #feedback is from you, asking "how's everyone finding it?". Now batch two shows up and the first thing they see is that.
 Same 25 people on email: you send a short update on a Thursday, "shipped CSV import, the date bug from last week is fixed, still slow on big files. Question: what did you try to do this week that didn't work?". Even if only 6 reply, that's 6 real answers in your inbox, and nobody else can see the other 19 stayed quiet.
 When I'd break the rule:
 users keep asking how other people set it up (they want each other, not you)
the product is something people build on, like templates, plugins or an API, where sharing is the point
you can honestly show up in it every day. If you can't, it'll go quiet again
One more thing, a server also splits your attention. Early on, all the signal you get should land in one place you actually read.
 The update email part is the bit that does the heavy lifting, and there's a decent write-up on writing founder updates early customers actually read on the EarlyHunt blog.
 Curious if anyone here had the opposite experience. Did a Discord actually work for you before you had, say, 100 users? What made people talk in it?

 A waitlist with no date on it is just a newsletter nobody asked for
Opinion I keep coming back to when I look at early-access pages: if the confirmation says "we'll let you know soon", the person has basically subscribed to a newsletter they didn't want. "Soon" means nothing to them. Two months later they get an invite, don't remember signing up, and the email goes to archive.
 So my rule now for any early access list: every person who signs up gets a window, not a "soon".
 Not a fake countdown. A real, slightly boring window, like "first batch goes out the week of 9 November, about 20 people, you're number 34 so probably the second batch, late November". If that slips, I email and say it slipped and why.
 Why I think this matters more than the waitlist design itself:
 People plan around dates. Someone who knows it's late November will actually keep the problem in mind. Someone told "soon" forgets in a week.
It forces me to be honest about batch size. If I can't write a date, it usually means I haven't decided how many people I can support yet. That's a me problem, not a marketing problem.
A slipped date is still a touchpoint. "We pushed batch two by a week because the import broke on big files" is a better email than silence, and people reply to those.
Worked example. Say you have 140 signups and you can handle about 20 new users a week without drowning in support. That's 7 weeks to get through the list. So the confirmation email for person number 90 says "you're roughly in batch five, around mid December. If you want in earlier, reply with what you'd use it for first and I'll look." That last line does two jobs, it lets the people with a real use case jump the line, and it gives you their first task before they even log in.
 Things I'd skip:
 referral "move up the list" games. They fill the list with people who wanted the position, not the product
showing the total count when it's small. "You're #34 of 41" is fine honestly, but don't fake it to look bigger
a date you already know you'll miss
There's a fuller write-up on keeping an early-access list warm until launch day on the EarlyHunt blog if you want the follow-up emails part.
 Curious what others do. Do you give waitlist people a real date, or does committing to one scare you off?
