WHERE BUFFERING ACTUALLY COMES FROM
Almost every stall gets blamed on the broadband, and most of the time that is wrong.
Video copes badly with unevenness, not with slowness. A line showing a healthy average that drops for half a second every minute produces exactly the freezing people blame on the provider. The opposite is true too: a modest but steady connection streams beautifully.
The five-minute diagnosis: while the stream is stuck, open something else on another device. If it loads instantly, your line is fine and the problem is upstream. Then look at the clock. A connection does not degrade at 8pm on a Saturday. An oversold shared server does.
THE TWENTY-MINUTE TEST THAT SETTLES IT
Do it at 3pm on a Saturday or on a European night. Not a Tuesday afternoon — everything works then and you learn nothing.
Open the channels from your own list, not the ones the app pushes on its home screen. Leave it running for thirty minutes without touching it; stalls come in clusters and a five-minute sample hides them entirely.
Then start a second stream in another room and watch the first screen, not the second. If the picture degrades within a minute, the ceiling belongs to the provider and no router tweak will fix it.

Stalls at peak time: effectively none on the service I kept, across months of Saturday afternoons and European nights.
What made the difference: multiple servers and being able to switch between them myself. Nothing else came close.
What made no difference: the channel count, the 4K badge on the sales page, and the headline price.
What I would do again: run the twenty-minute peak-time test before paying. What I would not: judge anything on a quiet weekday.
