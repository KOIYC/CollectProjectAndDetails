November 8, 2026
 Payments said no twice. So I built a town.
What is Tablif?
 Tablif gives every launched product its own office in a 3D town. Founders claim their office, change its design, and verify their site to show the office really belongs to them. There is also a game in the town, with seasons and weekly progress.
 What did I write in the first part?
 Tablif started as a stock market for launches, where people invested virtual money. Then I started collecting launches every day. But a launch was a one-day flame, and we were no different from other launch sites. I started using AI to read products' websites and produce competitor analysis and leads. The results were useful, but the people who came wanted to add their own product to the list, and then they left.
 Not getting approval from my payment provider for the lead generation service was a big disappointment. I only learned it after reading their documents. Until then I had no idea. I quickly looked at other providers, and a few of them had similar rules. I live in Turkey, so I can't use Stripe, and the providers I can use are limited.
 But competitor analysis had no such restriction. I had put a lot of work into both modules and did not want to throw them away. I decided to set the leads aside and keep the competitor analysis.
 There are many projects that do competitor analysis. I did not want to join them as one more. I thought I could keep fun and competitor analysis together, so I wanted to make it a bit of a game.
 If I built a city like SimCity and showed every founder their competitors there, the competitor analysis would not go to waste. I built it 2D first, then 3D. Every project has its own office. They can change the design, and they can even move their office next to the lake. I was going to show them their competitors there every day.
 The payment provider blocked me here too. This time they said "you can't sell listings" :) I stopped working with them on new projects. Selling listings was never my goal. The city was the fun part. The competitor analysis still runs in the background, but it is not part of the plans right now.
 A few days later, something caught my eye. Two people who had registered were coming in every other day. They edited their office, walked around, and then came back again.
 This reminded me of the people in the first idea, the stock market. In both versions, most of the people who came did not use the features. They tried to submit their own projects. The new people were decorating their own offices.
 I am a founder too, so I tried to understand them. Weeks, maybe months of work went into something, and they want to put it in front of people. What if I added a game to the city, and the game made their products more visible, here and in AI answers? Would they put in more effort?
 So I went back to gamification. A few things I had read about, and seen when I made games in the past, stayed in my mind:
 First, people value what they put effort into more (the IKEA effect). The people decorating their offices were doing exactly that.
 Second, people who feel they have already started finish more often than people starting from zero (the endowed progress effect, shown in a car wash card experiment: people whose card already had two stamps completed it noticeably more often).
 Third, most studies on whether gamification works find positive results, but the effect changes a lot with the user and the context. So a game does not work on its own. You have to try it and measure it.
 Most of my ideas did not make sense. The last one was an arena where projects fight each other. I dropped it, because it would feel like making little of the work founders put in.
 Then I remembered Travian. It is a text-based game I played a lot as a kid, where you build and grow your own village. I don't know if it is still active. The city was already there, so I built Travian's logic on top of it. There should be no war. We all pick a path and use it to make our projects visible. What if we did the same here, and grew our work here?
 I built the mechanics without asking anyone. I think that is one of my biggest mistakes. If you have even a small audience, talk to them.
 I built the game with a season structure. A season lasts 8 weeks, and the first season started on October 13. At the end of each week, that week's progress closes: the top spots and weekly rewards are announced. The home page also has a "this week in town" band. It shows the offices opened this week, newly verified offices, and the ones that grew the most. The goal is that someone who came on day one and someone who joined later can both see their own progress.
 After I opened the game, the number of players at each weekly close grew: 44 in the first week, 89 in the second, 115 in the third. But that number is misleading. Most people who open the game play for 2 or 3 days and quit. After the fourth day, their moves start to look alike. The core is small, and one person makes more than half of all the moves. The game gets people in. Something else brought them back.
 That something else was content. We added pages from the founder's own site to the offices. Once the founder approves, the page goes live and AI reads it. The more content an office has, the more AI reads it. An office with more than 15 pieces of content got about 18 AI reads in two weeks. An office with none got 3 or 4. The "your page is live" email also brought back about twice as many people as the game email.
 The real problem with the game now is sameness. I need to write new mechanics and make the second half of the season more varied.
 I would like to leave a note on this post: I built this city for you, but I built it without asking you. If you played, write what was boring and what was fun. If you did not play, write why. I will build the next season based on these comments.

 Tablif started as a stock market for launches. Then my payment provider said no.
Part 1 of 5: how Tablif became a town.
 Tablif started with a simple idea. A launch disappears after one day. What if launches worked like a stock market? People could make virtual investments in new products, and a product would stay visible for longer than its launch day.
 So we started collecting every new launch, every day, and built the system around it.
 After a while I noticed something. A launch is a one-day flame. It burns bright and it's gone. And with the stock market idea, we weren't really doing anything different from the launch sites themselves. We were just another board.
 So I changed direction. We had all these launches and their websites, so we used AI to read them and turned Tablif into a tool for competitor analysis and finding leads. Every day it showed you the likely customers and the rivals of your project.
 The results were interesting. I checked them many times, and a lot of them were genuinely useful.
 But then I noticed something else. Most people who signed up didn't come for rivals or customers. They wanted their project added to the launch lists we were collecting from. And once they were on the list, they left. Very few people came for the analysis itself.
 I didn't change direction. I kept going.
 Until our payment provider rejected the project. Because of how it was built, on top of lead and marketing data, they wouldn't approve it for payments.
 That was the day Tablif Town was born.
